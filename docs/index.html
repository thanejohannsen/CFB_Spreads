In-depth: how this tool works

How this works. Kalshi quotes a two-sided ladder of “team wins by over X.5 points” markets on each game. Each price is a probability, so the ladder is a full margin-of-victory distribution — the chance a side covers any number is read straight off the market rather than from a power rating.

Two clocks, one board. Both master records carry every pick the Master tab makes — neither filters on market quality — and differ only in when they stop moving. The headline locks an hour before each kickoff, when Kalshi’s volume peaks: open interest grew a median of 80% between Thursday and kickoff, and on one game 4,618%. That measures the method at its sharpest, not the picks you could actually submit on Wednesday night; the Thursday noon record, one click down, is that one.

Live, then locked. Until a record’s clock reaches a game, its row is the board: marked LIVE, moving with the market, and saying when it will freeze. After that it never changes again — PENDING while it waits on a final score, then the result. A live row counts toward no record; only a graded pick is a win or a loss. Click any record to see the individual picks behind it.

Where to place it. When Master or Kalshi ML vs Spread calls a side, the card ranks the ways to actually get on it. A Kalshi contract settles at $1, so its all-in price is its break-even — no odds conversion, and both venues compare in one unit. Three things to know. The sportsbook price is assumed at −110, because CFBD publishes the number but not the juice; correct it on the card and the ranking can change. Resting a limit beats taking the offer by about 2.4¢ — more than the edge the master usually finds — but only if you get filled. And a quote with a fraction of a contract behind it is struck through: Kalshi seeds price levels with hundredths of a contract and reports them as the market.

A cheaper price is not always a saving. Two things separate the venues: what each charges over its own midpoint, which is execution and is small, and the two of them pricing the same number differently, which is much larger and is a bet, not a discount — the one the Kalshi Spread vs Vegas Spread tab exists to measure and grade. The box splits them and says which is which, because folding the second into the first reports the tool’s own signal back as free money.

Two different things, two different colours. The grey badge is market quality — how precisely Kalshi is quoting that game, from S · Deep market down to D · Unreadable market. It describes the market, not the bet. The blue badge is pick strength — lean, solid, strong, set by the size of the edge. A deep market can still produce a lean, and a C-grade market is capped at lean however big the edge looks, because at that grade the number the edge was measured against is not solid enough to bet harder on.

Two clocks for the signals that earn them. The master is graded at Thursday noon and again an hour before kickoff, and Kalshi ML vs Spread now is too — it carries most of the master’s weight on a tight game, and Kalshi’s volume arrives late, so it is the signal most likely to read differently at the two moments. Both samples are still small enough that the gap between them means nothing yet; keeping it is how that changes.

Four tabs, six records. Master is the tool’s official pick and the one whose record counts. The other three each show a single comparison and keep their own separate record, using a looser rule — any edge over a point, with none of the master’s safety checks — because the point of a lens is to measure whether that signal carries information at all. Their definitions never change, so after a few weeks they show which signal is actually carrying the result.

The band matters more than the number. Deriving the implied spread twice, once from bids and once from asks, gives the market’s own uncertainty. When a line sits inside that band, the apparent edge is smaller than the noise it was measured against, and the tool says No play rather than inventing a pick.

Kalshi and Vegas are both reasonably efficient markets. Real edges are small and often explained by liquidity. This is a screening tool, not a guarantee — the graded record above is the honest test of whether it is worth anything.
